Sickle cell: a clear guide for families and professionals

What it is, how it’s passed on, the signs to know, how it’s treated in the UK, and how our community can help.

What is sickle cell?

Sickle cell disease is an inherited condition that affects red blood cells. Healthy red blood cells are round and flexible. In sickle cell disease they can become stiff and crescent-shaped (“sickled”), so they can get stuck in small blood vessels. That can cause pain, tiredness and damage to organs. It is usually lifelong, but treatment can help people live well.

It can affect people of any ethnicity, but it is more common in people with African, Caribbean, Mediterranean, South or Southeast Asian and Middle Eastern backgrounds.

≈19,000people live with sickle cell in the UK
150,000+babies are born with it in Nigeria every year
10–40%of people carry the gene in parts of Africa

How it’s passed on: trait, disease and your “genotype”

Many Nigerian families know this as the genotype question: AA, AS or SS. Here is what those letters mean.

AANo sickle gene
ASSickle cell trait (a carrier). You don’t have the disease and can’t develop it, but you can pass the gene on.
SSSickle cell disease (other types exist, such as SC)

Signs and sickle cell crises

Symptoms can start from around 6 months old and often come and go. The main ones are:

  • Pain in bones, joints, muscles or chest
  • Tiredness and weakness
  • Swollen hands and feet
  • Breathing problems
  • More frequent infections
  • Stomach pain

A sickle cell crisis is a sudden episode of severe pain or other symptoms. It can last hours, days or weeks. Common triggers include dehydration, sudden changes in temperature and stress. Over time, sickle cell can also affect the eyes, lungs, heart and kidneys, and can cause stroke, which is why regular check-ups matter.

Call 999 if someone with sickle cell has:

  • a crisis with severe pain, breathing difficulties or vomiting
  • a high temperature
  • a painful erection that won’t go away (priapism)

Don’t drive yourself to A&E. For anything less urgent, contact your GP or sickle cell team, or call NHS 111 (opens in new tab).

Screening in the UK

  1. In pregnancyA blood test is offered, ideally before 10 weeks. If the mother carries a gene, the baby’s father is offered a test too. It’s your choice.
  2. For newbornsSickle cell screening is part of the heel prick (blood spot) test offered to every baby.
  3. For adultsDon’t know your genotype? You can ask your GP for a carrier test at any time, for example before starting a family.

Treatment

Treatment is tailored to each person by their sickle cell team. Depending on their needs, it may include:

Everyday carePainkillers during a crisis, and antibiotics (mainly for children) and vaccinations to help prevent serious infections.
HydroxycarbamideA daily medicine (also called hydroxyurea) that may help reduce painful crises.
Blood transfusionsSome people may need transfusions, including red cell exchange, which replaces sickled cells with healthy donor cells. This is where closely matched blood matters.
Transplant and gene therapyFor a small number of people, a stem cell or bone marrow transplant may offer a cure, but it isn’t suitable for everyone. A one-off gene-editing therapy (exa-cel, Casgevy) may be an option in England through NICE managed access, for some people aged 12 and over with recurrent severe crises who could have a stem cell transplant but have no matched related donor.

Living well with sickle cell

Do
  • Drink plenty of fluids
  • Keep warm and avoid sudden temperature changes
  • Keep vaccinations up to date
  • Do gentle to moderate exercise, like walking or yoga, and avoid very intense exercise
  • Go to regular check-ups and follow your care plan
Don’t
  • Smoke
  • Drink alcohol

Myths and facts

Myth “Sickle cell trait is a mild form of the disease.”

Fact: People with the trait (AS) don’t have sickle cell disease and can’t develop it. They can pass the gene to their children.

Myth “You can catch sickle cell.”

Fact: It isn’t infectious. It is inherited from both parents.

Myth “Only people of African heritage are affected.”

Fact: People of any ethnicity can carry the gene. It’s most common in African and Caribbean communities, and also found in people with Mediterranean, South and Southeast Asian and Middle Eastern backgrounds.

Myth “Nothing can be done.”

Fact: Medicines, transfusions and good everyday care help people live well. Some people may be cured through a transplant or gene therapy, although long-term evidence for gene therapy is still being gathered.

Why Black heritage blood donors matter

Many people with sickle cell need regular transfusions of closely matched blood. Nearly half of Black heritage donors have the Ro blood subtype, which is rare in the wider population, yet just 3% of donors are from Black communities.
