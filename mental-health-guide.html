Looking after your own mental health: a guide for healthcare professionals

You look after everyone else. This guide is about you: spotting stress and burnout early, the pressures many of us carry, small things that help, and free, confidential support in England, Scotland and Wales.

Need help right now?

  • If your life is at risk, or you might act on thoughts of suicide: call 999 or go to A&E.
  • To talk to someone any time: call Samaritans free on 116 123, 24 hours a day.
  • Prefer to text? Text SHOUT to 85258, free and 24/7.
  • For urgent mental health help that isn’t an emergency: call NHS 111 (NHS 24 on 111 in Scotland).

Thoughts of suicide can happen to anyone in deep distress, and people do get through them. Telling someone is the first step.

A quick check-in: where are you today?

Mental health moves up and down for everyone, healthcare professionals included. Find where you are now, and the step that fits.

Stress or burnout?

Some stress is a normal part of demanding work. Burnout is what can happen when work stress goes on too long without enough recovery. The World Health Organization describes three signs:

  1. ExhaustionFeeling drained or empty, even after a day off.
  2. DistanceFeeling cynical or detached from work, patients or colleagues.
  3. DoubtFeeling you’re not doing your job well any more.

If two or three of these sound familiar and have lasted for weeks, it’s a signal to act, not a sign of weakness.

Pressures many of us carry

When we asked members what they wanted to talk about, these came up again and again. If any of them sound like you, you’re not alone.

Work-life balanceExtra shifts, long commutes and little time for yourself or family.
BurnoutShort-staffed wards and clinics, and the feeling you can never stop.
Money and the pressure to succeedBills here, family needs back home, and expectations to achieve and provide.
Guilt and boundariesFinding it hard to say no to work, church, community or relatives.
Feeling undervaluedBeing overlooked, or facing racism or unfair treatment at work. Your Freedom to Speak Up guardian, staff network or union can help.
Cultural attitudes“Just pray about it”, “be strong”, or worry about what people will say.

Small things that help

Try this week
  • Take your breaks, and eat and drink during shifts
  • Plan one proper rest day with no extra shifts
  • Keep a steady sleep routine, even after nights
  • Move: a 10-minute walk in daylight counts
  • Tell one person how you’re really doing
  • Write down your money worries and get free advice early
Watch out for
  • Using alcohol to switch off
  • Skipping leave “because the team needs me”
  • Saying yes to everything
  • Suffering in silence

Saying no without guilt

Boundaries protect your patients too, because a rested professional is a safer one. A few phrases you can borrow:

  • “I can’t take that shift, but I can help find someone.”
  • “I can’t send money this month. I can send something next month.”
  • “I’d love to help, but I’m resting this weekend.”
  • “Let me check my rota and come back to you.”

Myths and facts

Myth “Strong people don’t need help.”

Fact: Anyone can struggle, especially in demanding jobs. Getting help early is a sign of strength and good professional judgement.

Myth “It’s spiritual, so I just need to pray harder.”

Fact: Faith and community can be a real comfort, and many people find them helpful. They can sit alongside professional support, not instead of it.

Myth “If I ask for help, work or my regulator will find out.”

Fact: The staff support services below are confidential, and many let you refer yourself without telling your employer. If you’re worried about your registration, you can also speak in confidence to your union or professional body.

Myth “Therapy isn’t for people like us.”

Fact: Talking therapies help people of every background. If it matters to you, you can look for a therapist who shares or understands your culture (see BAATN below).

Free, confidential support for healthcare staff

These services are run for people who work in health and care. You can usually refer yourself without going through your employer.

EnglandNHS Practitioner HealthFor anyone working in the NHS in England who can’t get confidential care locally. Call 0300 0303 300 (weekdays) or text NHSPH to 85258 for crisis support.practitionerhealth.nhs.uk (opens in new tab)
ScotlandWorkforce Specialist ServiceConfidential mental health care for regulated health and social care professionals. Call 0300 0303 300. Everyone in health and care can also use the National Wellbeing Hub (wellbeinghub.scot (opens in new tab)).Workforce Specialist Service (opens in new tab)
WalesCanopiFree mental health support for NHS and social care staff in Wales (formerly HHP Wales). You can refer yourself online.canopi.nhs.wales (opens in new tab)

By profession, UK-wide

  • Doctors: BMA wellbeing support24/7 counselling line 0330 123 1245, open to all doctors, BMA member or not (opens in new tab)
  • Nurses: RCN counsellingFree, confidential counselling for RCN members (opens in new tab)
  • Nurses, midwives and HCAs: Cavell Nurses’ TrustCharity support, including when money is tight (opens in new tab)
  • Pharmacy: Pharmacist SupportFree counselling, peer support and financial help (opens in new tab)
  • UNISON members: There for YouGrants, debt advice and wellbeing breaks, useful for HCAs and support workers (opens in new tab)
  • Laura Hyde FoundationMental health support for medical and emergency services staff (opens in new tab)
  • BAATN therapist directoryFind a Black, African or Asian therapist who understands you (opens in new tab)

In England you can also refer yourself to NHS Talking Therapies (opens in new tab) for anxiety and depression. In Scotland and Wales, start with your GP. Everyone can also call a national helpline: Breathing Space in Scotland on 0800 83 85 87 (evenings and weekends), or the C.A.L.L. helpline in Wales on 0800 132 737 (24/7).

Work in social care or a private care home in England? NHS Practitioner Health is for NHS staff, but Samaritans, Shout, NHS 111 and NHS Talking Therapies are all open to you.

Worried about a colleague?

Check in privately and listen. If you’re worried they may be thinking about suicide, ask them directly: “Are you thinking about suicide?” Asking won’t put the idea in their head, and it can be a relief to be asked. Then help them reach one of the services on this page.
